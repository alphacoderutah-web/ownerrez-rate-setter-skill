# Pricing judgment: what to set, and the mistakes that cost real money

The guard makes changes *safe*. It cannot make them *right*. Read this when you're deciding
what number to set, rather than how to set it.

Every item below comes from a real portfolio where it actually happened.

---

## 1. Base price is a global lever

A base cut moves **every date**, including next year's peak. It is the wrong instrument for
a single soft month.

**What went wrong:** a five-bedroom home had a weak September. The response was a 19% base
cut plus a 53% promotion — and the promotion landed on *peak July dates*. Realised weekly
rate on comparable peak nights fell from about $7,000 to $2,889. The September problem was
real; the tool used to fix it was not.

**Instead:** fix one soft month with a date-limited override or a channel promotion scoped
to those dates. Leave the base alone unless the *whole* demand picture has changed.

**And watch the anchor.** PriceLabs' recommended base drifts down as you leave peak season.
Following it writes the seasonal decline into your base, which the seasonal profile then
applies on top of — you discount twice. Worse, base anchors next summer. Someone has to own
raising it back.

---

## 2. Check the fees before you cut the rent

Every OTA bundles fees into the nightly price a guest sees. So your guest-facing price is
`rent + fees`, but the only number PriceLabs controls is `rent`.

**What went wrong:** a $95/night "property care" fee was added to three properties without
telling the person managing rates. Guest-facing price rose about $700 on a week-long stay
while the rent line never moved. Conversion collapsed. The rate manager checked reviews,
checked listing health, found nothing, and cut base prices 8–19% chasing a problem that was
never in the rent.

**Before proposing a cut, ask:** has the fee structure changed? Compare recent bookings
against older ones — if fee-per-stay went up while rent stayed flat, the fee is your problem
and cutting rent will only lower your anchor.

**Three quantities, never conflated:**

| Quantity | What it is | What it drives |
|---|---|---|
| Rent | what PriceLabs controls | your lever |
| Guest-facing | rent + fees, ex-tax | **conversion** |
| Net | guest-facing − channel commission | what you bank |

Judging performance on rent alone understated one portfolio's value by 24%.

---

## 3. Rolling windows hide collapsed months

A 30- or 60-day pacing window blends months. A unit can read "59% occupancy, looks fine"
while a single month inside that window is at **zero**.

**What went wrong:** one property showed 59% on the 30-day window while September stood at
0 of 30 nights sold. The rolling view could not see it.

**Instead:** look at forward occupancy **month by month**, not just rolling windows. A month
under ~30% with real inventory left is a hole worth acting on.

**But scale it to your market's booking window.** An empty month 60 days out is normal where
most bookings arrive inside 15 days, and alarming where half arrive 60+ days ahead. Compute
your own booking-window mix before setting a threshold.

---

## 4. Seasons are learned, not assumed

"Summer is peak" is wrong for a third of some portfolios. One desert market peaks in
**October and November**, with summer as its *low* season — too hot. Derive peak and
shoulder from your own realised nightly rates per market, and never discount peak inventory
to fix a shoulder month.

---

## 5. Channel discounts stack on top of your floor

Your minimum price protects the rate PriceLabs *pushes*. Airbnb and Vrbo discounts apply on
top of that, so the rate a guest actually pays can land **below your floor** with nothing
showing in PriceLabs.

**What went wrong:** a Vrbo booking carried a 10% mobile promotion and a 12% length-of-stay
discount. Stacked, that's 22% off: $690 gross became $538 net against a $550 floor. Eight
distinct discount types were running at once.

**Instead:** periodically audit realised nightly rates from your booking records against each
unit's minimum. Nothing in PriceLabs will tell you.

---

## 6. Don't raise a price on a unit with nothing left to sell

A base raise only affects nights not yet booked. On a calendar that's effectively full, it's
noise — and if you're tracking your own recommendations, it manufactures a false disagreement
with whoever is declining to act on it.

---

## 7. Owner-occupied nights are not failures to sell

If an owner blocks their own property, those nights were never sellable. Counting them in
the denominator makes a healthy unit look catastrophic.

**What went wrong:** a property showed 2% occupancy against a 20% market — apparently the
most urgent problem in the portfolio. The owner uses it roughly half the year. Robust
statistics, a large comparable set, and a confidently wrong answer for want of one fact.

**Instead:** exclude blocked nights from the denominator, and get forward owner-use dates in
writing. They cannot be inferred until after they appear.

---

## 8. Compare like with like

Two traps that produce impressive, meaningless numbers:

- **Onboarding inflation.** A portfolio that grew from 4 to 15 units will show enormous
  year-over-year growth that has nothing to do with pricing. One market appeared to be up
  ~300%; restricted to units live in *both* years, it was **+1.4%**.
- **Unweighted forward averages.** Months six or more out hold almost no bookings, so their
  ratios explode. One portfolio's scorecard read **−20.5 points** on a flat average and
  **+5.4 points** revenue-weighted. Same data, opposite conclusion.

Always same-store, always revenue-weighted or occupancy-gated.

---

## 9. Observe long enough before concluding anything

Rate changes often arrive in batches every few days. An eight-day observation window that
happens to fall between two batches will look like a deliberate hold.

Before concluding someone is ignoring a recommendation, confirm you've watched across at
least two of their update cycles.

---

## 10. A denial is information

When the guard refuses a change, that refusal is usually right: the change was too large, too
soon after the last one, or on a unit with no authority. Each of those limits exists because
the alternative went badly somewhere.

The instinct to find a flag that makes the refusal go away is the single most expensive
instinct in this whole workflow.
